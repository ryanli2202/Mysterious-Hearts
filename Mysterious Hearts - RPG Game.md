# Mysterious Hearts: RPG Game

A text RPG romance simulation with four playable male protagonists. Choose Garrett Han, Rei Takamura, Minsu Park, or Ivan Yeun. Garrett and Rei are best friends.

This document separates established character canon from proposed game design. Canon includes details selected at the user's explicit request, identified where relevant. Proposed material is not confirmed canon.

## Confirmed setting

The game is set in Winterfield, a city in the United States. Garrett lives in the hills, while Rei lives in the centre of downtown. The game uses the [Winterfield world reference](Mysterious%20Hearts%20-%20Worldbuilding.md) for local life and calendar occasions. The [character conversation tables](Mysterious%20Hearts%20-%20Character%20References.md) guide what each speaker can say to each protagonist as their bond changes.

Mysterious Hearts takes place in an alternate universe of Vendetta. Awakened and rare Traits exist under a unified United Nations world government. Rei combines Flowing Tempest with footwork and timing. Garrett learned to fight on Hong Kong streets, and the brother who once beat him still knows his habits better than anyone.

## Confirmed character canon

### Ivan Yeun

Ivan is 24, stands 193 cm tall, and celebrates his birthday on March 4. His MBTI is ISTP. Onstage he can hold a stadium through the silence before a chorus. Offstage he would happily spend an afternoon alone, taking a sticking piano key apart. He rehearses until he can land either half of a duet, a habit Valentina exploits whenever karaoke night needs a last-minute partner.

His mother used to keep the kitchen radio on while he trained. Ivan first worked out how to sing harmony by following the bass line between rounds. Years of training in striking, throws, grappling and weapons made him a master of all martial arts. Choreographers hired him to teach performers how to fall. One of them heard him sing while they reset the mats and offered him a microphone.

By day he is a famous K-pop idol with rehearsals, recording sessions and modelling shoots. By night he works as the Syndicate’s Joker Card, a high executive trusted to settle disputes between its branches. He took their money to get out of an abusive early management contract. He stayed long enough to sign off on threats himself. He wants out and knows some of the people he hurt will still want answers after he leaves. His criminal work is private. Familiarity with his music gives an acquaintance no knowledge of his rank.

Freedom matters most to him. He keeps one hour on his calendar blank and writes songs there that nobody has bought. Protecting his family can bring out his worst habit, making a decision on someone else’s behalf. His younger adoptive sister Valentina once made him sit through an entire boat repair with his hands in his pockets after he tried to dismiss the mechanic she had hired. These days he asks whether she wants him to bring tools or lunch. They trust each other deeply and share a strictly familial bond.

Ivan met Garrett during a criminal deal, Rei when a K-pop campaign paired him with a model, and Minsu when Ivan tried to recruit him into the Syndicate. Minsu’s participation was never agreed. The others came into his life through introductions by those three. Each acquaintance knows the circumstances they personally witnessed.

He taps a four-count against his thumb while waiting and checks his watch against the last harbour ferry. In texts he uses short sentences, gives a real time for an invitation, and lets the other person choose. Valentina gets the unpolished voice notes. “Second verse is yours. Yes, even the high bit.”

Ivan and Valentina live together in the Ferry Signal House. The old signal windows are low enough for Valentina to watch the evening boats from her sail-mending bench. Across the room, Ivan’s walnut-lined piano alcove holds a pair of headphones for the nights he comes home with a song after she has gone to bed. They have separate bedrooms beneath the reading mezzanine. Their perpetual argument is over the scarred oak table. She needs the full length for a sail. He needs exactly enough space for two bowls of soup.

Mina Cho, Ivan’s personal driver, brings a midnight-blue Bentley Flying Spur Mulliner to the ferry steps. He usually takes the rear seat and marks up a score between appointments. A little sailboat from Valentina hangs from the mirror. Mina brings the passenger van for a larger party.

Within the Syndicate, Ivan holds the Black and White Joker Card. His affiliates operate across Russia and Asia. His adoptive sister Valentina is one of his five generals, the Queen of Wind, with the Deathwind Trait. Both siblings already know their command roles; those roles remain private around outsiders. He answers to the Cardmaster and can call on all Four Suits. His fellow executive Sylvanis holds the Coloured Joker Card and serves as second-in-command. When Ivan asked to leave, Sylvanis showed him an order bearing Ivan’s signature and asked which newspaper should get the original. Ivan still has Sylvanis’s number. He would recognise that voice anywhere.

At sixteen, Garrett united all the high schools in Hong Kong through martial prowess alone. Beneath his outgoing personality, he is secretly sad about losing his parents and is looking for Charles.

#### Growing up with Charles

After their parents died, Charles tried to make their mother's noodles from the instructions on the back of an electricity bill. She'd written “enough water” and apparently considered the matter settled. The brothers ate the first batch with spoons. For the second, Garrett measured the water in their father's chipped teacup while Charles kept his hands in the dough. They added their own figures to the bill. Years later Garrett can still hear Charles saying, “Your thumb was inside the cup last time. Put it back.”

Charles worked early shifts. Getting himself to school gave Garrett plenty of time to decide whether he felt like going. He loved martial arts practice and would cross Hong Kong for a chance to spar with somebody good. He also liked the ten minutes after a fight when everybody wanted to walk beside him. By sixteen he'd beaten the toughest student each school could put up. Boys he'd never met were slipping out of class to follow the Flaming Prince. His childhood friend April was still willing to beat him at chess in front of them. He once asked her to let him resign gracefully. She made him tip his own king over.

#### Seventeen, behind Mrs Lau's shop

The evening Charles beat him up, Garrett was sitting on an overturned drinks crate eating a free pear. Earlier that afternoon a delivery boy had asked Garrett's friends to move their bikes from the entrance to Mrs Lau's fruit shop. Garrett had hooked a foot around the boy's trolley as he pulled it past. Two trays of pears went down the kerb. His friends were still telling the story when Charles arrived in his work shirt and picked a price sticker off Garrett's sleeve.

“How much do you owe her?”

Garrett looked at his audience. “Put it on my account.”

Charles took the pear out of his hand. “Get up.”

Garrett got up grinning. He'd spent a year waiting for somebody to give him a decent fight. He caught Charles cleanly across the cheek. Charles crowded him against the shutter, hit him twice in the ribs and put him down. Garrett scrambled up and tried again. A minute later he was back on the concrete with a split lip, one eye closing and absolutely no desire to get up a third time. Charles sat beside him, breathing hard. The boys gathered their bikes. One remembered to take the empty crate back inside.

“I used to send you here with a note for groceries,” Charles said. “Today she rang me to ask if it was safe to send that kid back for his trolley.”

Garrett had an excellent answer ready for anyone who called him a delinquent. He had no answer for Mrs Lau having to make that call. After a while he asked Charles where he'd learned to fight like that.

“Same place you did. You think they opened the gym the day you turned up?”

Garrett laughed and his lip opened again. For years afterward, whenever somebody asked who'd given the Flaming Prince his worst beating, he answered “My big brother” with unmistakable pride. Charles had walked alone into a whole crowd wearing Garrett's colours and taken him on. Garrett admired him for that. He also remembered that Charles made him go into the shop alone to settle up. Mrs Lau took his money and told him the delivery boy's name was Kit. Apologising to Kit the following morning, without Charles there to get him started, was the part Garrett found hard.

#### The last call from Hong Kong

At eighteen Garrett moved from Hong Kong to Winterfield with his childhood friend April to work in a restaurant kitchen. His knife skills impressed the owner considerably less than his ability to seat twelve impatient customers while a burner was out. He liked both sides of the counter and began saving for a place where he could run both. On Sundays Charles phoned from Hong Kong during prep. In their last conversation they argued about whether their mother had toasted her shrimp shells for broth. Garrett cut the call short when a supplier knocked. The next Sunday he had a pot ready for comparison and nobody to argue with.

When Charles missed a second call, Garrett rang his old workplace and learned he'd left his job. Friends in Hong Kong checked his flat. Somebody had collected his clothes. Nobody Garrett trusted could tell him who. At the start of the game he still wants the ordinary end of that unfinished conversation as badly as he wants an explanation. The reason Charles disappeared and his present whereabouts are unknown.

#### Life at twenty-two

At the Golden Dragon, Garrett gives the first bowl from a fresh batch to whichever staff member has time to eat it hot. He remembers the dishwasher's exam dates and can keep a table of strangers talking through a kitchen delay. A regular once brought in a date expressly to meet him and spent the meal regretting it. Garrett caught on halfway through, excused himself to the kitchen and sent out two bowls with the dumplings folded into slightly lopsided hearts. He can be generous and vain in the space of a single lunch.

Rei first came in after a modelling shoot and drew the one dumpling Garrett had split. These days Garrett can tell how a service went by what's on the back of Rei's receipt. His favourite sketch is of his own hand stealing a dumpling off Rei's plate. It's taped inside the till drawer. When a lead about Charles dries up, Garrett sometimes texts him three words after closing. “Hungry tonight, bro?” Rei knows Garrett keeps enough dough for two.

After dark Garrett runs the Red Dragons from a private lounge. He likes being the man people come to for a decision, a pleasure he recognises from sixteen and still has trouble giving up. One evening a young runner laughed at a joke Garrett knew was lousy. Watching the boy check his face for permission to stop, Garrett heard the laugh from behind Mrs Lau's shop again. He cut the meeting short. He still went back the following night. Which parts of the Flaming Prince he is willing to leave behind is a live question for him.

At home in the hills he has a garage full of beautiful cars and a kitchen drawer that sticks because the old teacup is too tall for it. He could move it. He likes having to put his thumb inside the rim to get it out.

#### Meeting Rei, Emily and Sophie

They first met during the Golden Dragon’s early months, after Garrett had moved to Winterfield and Rei had begun modelling; neither was a schoolmate of the other. Rei came in alone after a shoot, carrying a sketchbook and wanting a late meal. Garrett offered to replace a split dumpling, but Rei kept it and drew its broken seam on the receipt. Garrett asked whether that was a complaint; Rei said it had more character than the intact ones. Garrett pinned up the drawing and remembered his order on the next visit. Repeated late meals became conversations about cooking and making things, then washing bowls together after closing. Garrett learned to leave a silence alone; Rei began arriving on evenings when he did not need dinner as an excuse. “Dumpling inspection” grew from that first receipt. The later drawing of Garrett stealing a dumpling is a separate sketch kept inside the till drawer.

Rei introduced them at the Golden Dragon a few weeks after he began returning regularly. Emily brought a small arrangement for the counter and checked that it would not crowd the service space. Garrett tried to move it where everyone could see it; she pointed out that steam would spoil it there. He moved it back and asked her to choose the spot. When he called himself the expert on everything behind his counter, she answered that the flowers had requested a second opinion. “The flowers have filed a complaint” became their occasional joke when his presentation outruns practicality. He later asked her directly about flowers instead of routing every invitation through Rei. They are friends in their own right, though Emily remains much closer to Rei; Garrett does not know her private crush.

Rei introduced them at a small house party later that same season, after Garrett had already met Emily. Garrett was confidently estimating how quickly he could put together food for the room; Sophie offered to time him. The food took longer than his boast, partly because he kept stopping to talk. Sophie announced “conversation time counts,” and Garrett insisted the audience had interfered with the athlete. It became a light recurring joke about his ambitious estimates, never a real competition result. He liked that she challenged the performance and still helped carry plates; she liked that he could lose a silly argument without sulking. Invitations to later parties made them friends. Much later Sophie introduced Minsu to both Garrett and Rei at another party; that was not their own first meeting.

Garrett therefore knows that broad history from Rei, not from criminal contacts or deduction. His established disclosure did not include the name Hamayashi-kai, the full extent of the torture, or Yuriko’s own account of misogynistic abuse; those details remain unknown to him unless explicitly shared. Rei also separately told him he fights at the Iron Circle; Garrett knows the venue and that it is private, but has not thereby witnessed a bout or learned its roster. Garrett knows the bus sketchbook, the backdrop-to-modelling start and the rice cooker from Rei’s stories, not from being present during school. He does NOT know that Rei killed the clan, the escape sequence, or the identities or fate of Rei’s biological parents. He does know about Marcus and Joseph’s bullying from a separate explicit disclosure by Rei after their friendship was established. Yuriko has not automatically been told what Rei disclosed to Garrett.

#### The evening Rei told Garrett about Marcus

Before the game, after their friendship was established, Rei stayed at the Golden Dragon after closing while Garrett folded clean towels. Rei had sketched a little paper bird on the back of a receipt. When Garrett asked about it, Rei told him about Marcus’s unfinished stop-motion pigeon film and the hand Marcus had called “a very local weather event.” Garrett laughed at the joke before realising Rei was speaking about someone he had lost. Rei then told him plainly that Marcus had been one of his closest school friends, that Joseph Richter and several classmates had repeatedly bullied him, and that Marcus later died by suicide. Rei said Joseph still treated the bullying as something that merely happened at school. He did not describe a method of death or present a single explanation for Marcus’s suicide. Garrett put the towel down, let Rei finish and asked whether he wanted him to listen or help with something. His first impulse was anger and a practical plan, but he did not make the conversation into a promise of retaliation. “He doesn’t get to make it small because he’s finished thinking about it,” he said. When Rei said he just wanted to talk, Garrett stayed, left the cleaning unfinished and asked what Marcus had wanted to do with the film. Rei told him about the miniature city and the impossible bus timetable. Garrett remembers both the cruelty Rei described and the funny, exacting person Marcus had been.

Garrett now knows Marcus’s name, that he was Rei’s close school friend, that Joseph and classmates repeatedly bullied him, that Marcus died by suicide, and that Joseph later minimised his conduct. His source is Rei’s explicit private disclosure before the game. Garrett never met Marcus and did not attend their school or witness the bullying; he says “Rei told me” or “the story you told me,” not “I remember when Marcus and I.” He knows the paper-pigeon story and miniature-city ambition as stories Rei told him, not shared childhood memories. This disclosure does not give him Marcus’s surname, a death method, private final words or a new explanation for the death. It also does not reveal Rei’s clan killings, Hamayashi-kai’s name, further torture details or Yuriko’s private account of abuse. The conversation is shared history between Garrett and Rei even if their current trust falls. Garrett does not casually retell Rei’s disclosure to outsiders or assume Emily and Sophie know that Rei told him.

Garrett feels enduring anger and contempt toward Joseph for bullying Marcus and minimising it. He trusts Rei’s account and does not require him to prove it again. When Rei brings Joseph up, Garrett’s usual easy charm becomes quieter and more deliberate; he checks what happened today and what Rei wants, rather than pushing for a confrontation. He can say, “I believe you. Do you want me beside you, or do you want some space?” If he meets Joseph, he can remain civil for a practical reason while being cold, brief and unimpressed by his wealth or title. He does not treat a useful answer as forgiveness or assume success means Joseph changed. His anger does not authorise threats, violence, an invented meeting or deciding Rei’s next action. In mixed company he can show distrust and offer a private conversation without broadcasting why. He can also ask Rei about Marcus’s film on another evening; Marcus need not be reduced to the reason Garrett dislikes Joseph.

#### Before Winterfield

Hamayashi-kai is the yakuza clan that raised Rei and Yuriko. From birth, Rei endured extreme training and torture intended to forge him into a human weapon. At fifteen he had to kill the entire clan to take Yuriko and escape; he then brought them both to the United States. Yuriko was twelve. She was given a much more sheltered life than Rei, but was still subjected to misogynistic ideals, control and abuse. Sheltered does not mean safe or unharmed. Their experiences were unequal, and neither sibling’s suffering cancels the other’s. Rei’s memory gaps remain: the outcome is established, while the exact sequence, methods and escape route are not.

In the clan house, the kitchen had the only clock Rei could hear from the training room. By the last round of practice he could tell how late dinner would be from the smell of the rice catching. The cook sometimes gave him the crisp layer from the bottom of the pot. Years later, in a kitchen of his own, he still leaves the heat on a little too long because he likes it that way.

The adults who raised him kept meticulous records of his bouts. Nobody kept his drawings. He drew on the paper bands around bundles of chopsticks, usually hands doing ordinary things such as peeling a pear or threading a needle. He could finish one in the time it took the cook to turn around. At fifteen he killed the clan that had trained him, rescued his younger sister Yuriko and got them both to the United States. The exact sequence of that night is missing from the account he can give. The kitchen and the paper bands belong to his history. A narrator can show them to the player without giving present-day Rei a clear memory of them.

#### Starting over at fifteen

During his first term in Winterfield, Rei carried a folded bus timetable in his pocket. He could manage the journey to school. What puzzled him was where people went when the last bell rang and nobody had told them where to be. For weeks he rode to the end of the line and back, sketching whoever sat across from him. His first Winterfield sketchbook ends with six pages of the same bus driver in increasingly elaborate hats. In December she finally caught him drawing and asked for the one with the antlers.

He and Emily became friends over a model greenhouse she was building for school. One afternoon he found two boys passing its little roof over her head. Rei held out his hand until one gave it back. Then he made a mistake. He started packing up the greenhouse for her. “I-I still have to present it,” Emily told him. He carried it back inside while she walked ahead with the roof. At her presentation he learned why she'd painted one pane white to shade the seedlings. By spring he was the person she trusted to hold a whole tray steady while she eased out one stubborn root. He still asks what she wants him to hold. He takes that ease between them for friendship.

#### Marcus

Marcus was one of Rei’s closest friends at high school in Winterfield and later died by suicide. Rei, Emily and Sophie already know his name and that he died; Emily and Sophie learned of his death during their school years, not from the player during the game. They can recognise a reference to Marcus, acknowledge the loss and ask whether Rei wants to talk or simply have company. They need not ask who Marcus is or react as if the death is new information. Emily and Sophie already know from their school years that Joseph and several classmates repeatedly bullied Marcus. This is established knowledge before the game, not a new allegation Rei must prove. No particular incident is newly established as something either woman personally witnessed. Do not invent Marcus’s surname, the method of death, a final message, a funeral scene or a single explanation for his suicide. He is a distinct person from Emily and this history is separate from her greenhouse incident and Rei’s pre-Winterfield memory gaps. Do not turn his death into an inside joke or force it into ordinary conversation.

**Personality and interests.** Marcus was curious, wry and quietly theatrical once he felt at home. He could spend a whole lunch arguing for a ridiculous idea in a perfectly serious voice, then lose his nerve when a stranger asked what he was talking about. He liked making his friends laugh, but also wanted them to take his unfinished work seriously. He loved stop-motion films, puzzle games, odd little mechanical objects and inventing titles for things. He carried a battered notebook full of storyboards, overheard phrases and drawings of imaginary ticket machines. He hoped to make a short film with a miniature city and an ordinary person getting spectacularly lost in it. He preferred cheese-and-pickle sandwiches, salt-and-vinegar crisps and cloudy apple juice, and argued that a slightly bruised apple was still a perfectly good lunch. He was generous with his ideas and reluctant to lend a favourite pen. He could be late because he kept improving something that was already good enough, get defensive about criticism, and sulk after losing a puzzle game. He usually came back with a better question and an awkward apology. His friends liked him without finding him easy every moment.

**An afternoon all four remember.** Shared by all four before Marcus’s death: on a rainy lunch break Marcus proposed a stop-motion film about a paper pigeon trying to buy a bus ticket. Rei sketched the pigeon, Emily built a cardboard shelter, Sophie carried a desk lamp between classrooms and Marcus moved the bird a little for every frame. They only finished a few seconds. Marcus left his hand in one frame and defended it as “a very local weather event.” They all laughed, including him. “Local weather” became a small joke about a hand, shadow or other accidental intrusion into a project; the joke is about making the film, never his death. He insisted the bus shelter needed a timetable even though it would be unreadable. Emily agreed, Rei drew it, and Sophie complained that even the paper bus was going to be late. They also remember a lunch-table puzzle game Marcus devised on graph paper: Sophie tried to race it, Emily found that one route had no exit, and Rei drew a bench for the stranded player. Marcus wrote “temporarily scenic” beside the dead end and eventually fixed it. They can remember the unfinished, funny afternoon without presenting the film or game as a secret final message.

**With Rei.** Rei’s friendship with Marcus grew at a school library table. Marcus asked whether the person in Rei’s sketch was waiting for somebody or merely resting; he was interested in the drawing before knowing much about its maker. They began exchanging tiny prompts on scrap paper: a room with no door, a station after the last train, a bird that refused to fly. Marcus supplied situations and Rei found their shapes. He was one of the people Rei could sit with without having to explain the silence. They disagreed about a story ending: Marcus wanted a spectacular reveal and Rei thought the empty room was enough. Marcus called it “a very expensive drawing of nothing”; Rei answered that the paper had been free. A few days later Marcus brought two endings and asked which felt honest rather than which was cleverer. Rei remembers that he could be stubborn and still come back to listen. Marcus once borrowed Rei’s eraser and returned it in a folded paper envelope labelled “temporary custody concluded.” Rei occasionally recalls the envelope when lending art supplies. Marcus did not know Rei’s Hamayashi-kai history merely because they were close. When Rei talks about him, let him miss the next idea Marcus would have argued for, or smile at his elaborate nonsense, without forcing every recollection into guilt.

**With Emily.** Emily had her own friendship with Marcus, not simply a place beside Rei. They became comfortable while sorting scrap card for school projects. Marcus held up two almost identical greens and asked whether she would notice if he used the wrong one; she immediately pointed to the cooler shade. Afterward he brought her colour decisions with exaggerated solemnity. He liked her exact answers and gave her time to finish them. During a miniature shop build he confidently made a door too small for the figure he had already chosen. Emily made him measure it rather than discard the figure; he labelled the corrected doorway “management apologises.” They used that phrase for a small fix after somebody had been too confident, with both free to laugh. She remembers him asking about her work when there was no audience, saving the straight scraps for her and occasionally losing the piece he had promised to bring. Once he pushed a joke about her exactness too far. She told him it stopped being funny when he kept repeating it; he apologised and stopped. Her affection includes that he could accept a boundary. She may recall the two green cards, an overcomplicated label or his stubborn preference for the scratched prop over the tidy one. She can laugh warmly, be annoyed all over again about a missing piece, or simply say she misses him. Her own grief need not become a speech reassuring Rei.

**With Sophie.** Sophie and Marcus first found an easy rhythm over their different approaches to school activities: she wanted to start, while he wanted a title, rules and a revised title. ## Proposed game design

### Format and core loop

A choice-driven text RPG with adult female romance interests. Selecting a protagonist changes the narration, dialogue, personal story, and approaches available in scenes.

Each day could have afternoon, evening, and late-night periods. Players divide their time between:

- Following personal-story leads, including Garrett's search for Charles and Rei's forgotten history.
- Building a life through work, cooking, training, artistic pursuits, and friendships.
- Developing relationships through messages, shared activities, dates, and difficult conversations.

Time commitments create consequences. Following a lead might require rescheduling a date; communicating honestly, disappearing without explanation, and involving a partner should lead to different responses.

### Relationship systems

Track attraction, trust, and emotional intimacy separately, alongside remembered choices and relationship agreements. Attraction need not imply trust, and friendship can precede romance. Commitment should develop through an explicit conversation.

Each woman has her own interests, ambitions, boundaries, and preferences. Flirting is not universally effective, and some relationships may remain friendships. Honest casual relationships and committed relationships can both be possible; broken promises have character-specific consequences.

Shared romance characters can have different introductions and relationship development for each protagonist while retaining consistent personalities and goals.
